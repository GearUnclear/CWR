# Research memo: readable 35–45-word character dossiers

Purpose: improve immediate readability and character specificity. The recommendations below are editorial heuristics, not a method for identifying or disguising AI writing. No source located studies 35–45-word in-game character bios directly; the strongest style studies analyze 150–500-word fiction or roughly 500-word genre-matched passages, so apply the findings cautiously at this shorter length.

## Primary research read

- Reinhart et al., “Do LLMs write like humans? Variation in grammatical and rhetorical styles,” *PNAS* (2025): https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422455122. The study compares human and LLM passages across six genres in parallel corpora, using 66 lexical, grammatical, and rhetorical measures. In this setup, instruction-tuned models often wrote in a noun-heavy, information-dense style. GPT-4o used present-participial clauses at 5.3× the human rate and nominalizations at 2.1×; it also overused words such as “tapestry,” “palpable,” and “intricate.” The paper stresses genre fit: these words are not inherently wrong, but can feel conspicuous in a given register. These are group-level tendencies tied to tested models and prompts, not categorical tells or a forbidden-word list. Its comparisons/classifiers mostly used ~500-word chunks, not bios.

- O’Sullivan, “Stylometric comparisons of human versus AI-generated creative writing,” *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (2025): https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05986-3. It compares 250 crowdsourced human short stories with 130 stories from GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Llama 3-70B, all written to the same two prompts and 150–500 words long. The human stories spread across broader stylistic clusters; outputs from each model clustered more tightly, with some overlap. This supports varying each dossier’s sentence shape and perspective according to the character, rather than applying one polished template to everyone. The paper itself cautions that its narrow prompts constrain the comparison.

- Callan & Foster, “How interesting and coherent are the stories generated by a large-scale neural language model?,” *Expert Systems* (2023): https://doi.org/10.1111/exsy.13292. Readers rated GPT-Neo stories for coherence, interest, and relation to the prompt; story coherence correlated strongly with story interest (r=.80), while automatic coherence metrics had low correlation with readers. The authors emphasize that believability and interest are subjective. For bios, this favors a direct, coherent link between the character’s defining situation and action, then a human read for whether it lands.

- Sadasivan et al., “Can AI-Generated Text be Reliably Detected?” (2023; arXiv preprint, updated 2025): https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11156. Their detector stress tests used passages of about 300 tokens; results varied under paraphrasing, and the paper gives theoretical limits when AI and human text distributions become similar. This does not establish detector behavior on 35–45-word bios. Treat style cues as tendencies in a sampled corpus, not proof about authorship; there is no need to optimize these bios against detectors.

## Practical rules for 35–45 words

1. Open with the character’s concrete role, place, or immediate problem. Give the reader an anchor before backstory or mood.
2. Use plain, active verbs and concrete nouns. Prefer what the person does, carries, risks, or refuses over abstract labels such as “determination,” “legacy,” or “a commitment to.”
3. Keep one clear actor and action in each sentence. Break up long modifier chains and repeated “-ing” clauses; use a direct clause when it carries the same meaning.
4. Spend the word budget on one defining fact, one motive or tension, and one consequential choice or detail. Cut facts that only repeat the character’s role.
5. Make the character specific through an observable detail, habit, tool, place, or decision. Avoid stacking generic virtues, ominous adjectives, or interchangeable dramatic claims.
6. Let the motive emerge from the facts; avoid ending with a broad moral, slogan, or abstract summation that could fit anyone.
7. Use two or three sentences with varied lengths and openings. Let the character’s temperament shape the rhythm; do not force every dossier into the same sentence pattern.
8. Read it once at normal speed. If the reader has to reread to identify who did what, split the sentence or replace the abstract phrase with a concrete action.

These rules target plain comprehension and believable characterization. They are editorial decisions, not reliable indicators of who wrote a passage.
