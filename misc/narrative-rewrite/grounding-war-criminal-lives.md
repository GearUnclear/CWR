# Grounded life patterns for fictional war-criminal biographies

This set uses seven cases from five conflicts. Build from court-established conduct, distinguish claims from findings, and leave private motives blank where records do. These histories are reference patterns, not templates for copying real people.

## 1. Adolf Eichmann — the administrative climber

Eichmann moved with his family from Germany to Linz, left mechanical-engineering training, and worked as a laborer, office worker, and traveling salesman. An acquaintance drew him into Austria’s Nazi Party and SS in 1932. He later led a Vienna office that forced Jewish emigration, then organized a failed mass-deportation experiment to Nisko. The [USHMM biography](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/adolf-eichmann) says superiors valued that initiative and assigned him further deportation work. As chief of RSHA IV B 4, he coordinated deportations across Europe; the Jerusalem court’s [verdict](https://www.theeichmanntrial.org/verdict) convicted him of crimes against the Jewish people, crimes against humanity, and war crimes. The record supports a career path from salesman to specialist to office chief, with institutional reward for initiative.

## 2. Kaing Guek Eav (“Duch”) — the teacher who built a security office

Born to a poor farming family, Duch became a mathematics teacher in 1965 and joined the underground resistance in 1967. After imprisonment, he rose through Party security, headed M-13, helped establish S-21, then became its chairman. The [ECCC profile](https://eccc.gov.kh/en/cases/charged-profile/kaing-guek-eav) and [appeal judgment](https://www.eccc.gov.kh/sites/default/files/AppealJudgment-w-annex_Eng.pdf) document his role designing procedures, training staff, transmitting orders, and annotating confessions. The court found he knowingly directed and enabled torture and executions; the appeal chamber imposed life imprisonment. It also found he used discretion to implement Party ideology, so “just following orders” is not an adequate summary.

## 3. Jean-Paul Akayesu — the local official whose conduct changed

Akayesu taught school, became a school inspector, entered local politics in 1991, and was elected bourgmestre (mayor) in 1993, with executive, public-order, and police authority. The [ICTR judgment](https://unictr.irmct.org/files/case-documents/ictr-96-4/trial-judgements/en/980902.pdf) records a sharp chronology: before 18 April 1994 he took some steps to protect residents and sought national assistance; afterward he stopped trying to prevent killings and participated in, supervised, ordered, and incited crimes. Judges found that on 19 April he urged a crowd to eliminate the “enemy,” understood in Taba as Tutsi. His convictions and life sentence were affirmed on appeal ([ICTR case record](https://unictr.irmct.org/en/news/appeals-chamber-upholds-sentences-akayesu-kayishema-and-ruzindana-trials)). Specify the date, authority, and choices; do not claim he was always openly violent or assign one emotion as the cause.

## 4. Jean Kambanda — the banker who became prime minister

An economist, insurance worker, and bank official, Kambanda had led the MDR party in Butare before becoming prime minister on 8 April 1994. The ICTR’s [summary of his admissions](https://unictr.irmct.org/en/news/ex-rwandan-prime-minister-jean-kambanda-pleads-guilty-genocide) records concrete decisions: distributing weapons knowing their use, ordering roadblocks to identify people for elimination, praising incitement on RTLM, and failing to answer when asked how to protect massacre survivors. He pleaded guilty to six counts, including genocide and incitement; life imprisonment was affirmed on appeal ([case record](https://unictr.irmct.org/en/cases/ictr-97-23)). The record offers no reliable private explanation for his choices.

## 5. Dražen Erdemović — a junior soldier facing a coercive order

Erdemović was 23 when he took part in the July 1995 executions at Branjevo farm. He had completed primary school and three years of technical secondary training as a locksmith. His account at sentencing described service in several armed forces during the war and said he did not want to fight; those details are recorded as his testimony in the [ICTY sentencing judgment](https://www.icty.org/x/cases/erdemovic/tjug/en/erd-tsj961129e.pdf). He admitted shooting unarmed men brought from Srebrenica as part of an execution squad. At his later guilty plea to a war crime, the Prosecutor accepted that he acted on a superior’s order under threat of death; the Chamber treated those circumstances as mitigation and sentenced him to five years. An earlier plea was set aside because the Appeals Chamber found it uninformed ([ICTY procedural and sentencing summary](https://www.icty.org/en/press/drazen-erdemovic-sentenced-5-years-imprisonment)). This case grounds a story of coercion without erasing agency: show who issued the threat, what the soldier did next, what escape or refusal seemed to cost, and the later decision to confess. Do not present the Tribunal’s ruling as finding that duress excused the crime.

## 6. Milan Babić — the dentist turned regional political leader

Before the Croatian conflict, Babić was a dentist. He became a prominent leader in the Serbian Democratic Party in Croatia, president of the Knin municipal assembly, and head of the self-declared SAO Krajina administration. In his guilty plea, he admitted sharing the objective of forcibly removing non-Serbs. The [ICTY sentencing judgment](https://www.icty.org/x/cases/babic/tjug/en/bab-sj290604e.htm) found that he used political authority to advance it: he helped establish governing bodies, planned and promoted policies, helped reorganize and recruit territorial forces, supplied political and material support, and made inflammatory speeches. He pleaded guilty to persecution as a crime against humanity and received 13 years; the Tribunal’s [case page](https://www.icty.org/case/babic/4) records the conviction. At sentencing, the Prosecution argued that he entered politics to protect Serbs and was promoted while loyal to stronger leaders, but this was a party submission, not a judicial finding of his inner motive. That distinction is useful: a character may repeat a public defense that fits his interests, while the story shows what he knowingly did.

## 7. Charles Taylor — the neighboring leader who supplied a rebellion

Taylor trained as a teacher, worked as an accountant, and studied accounting and economics in the United States. He returned to Liberia and held senior purchasing and commerce roles in Samuel Doe’s government; after leaving Liberia amid embezzlement accusations, he became a rebel leader and later president. His [Special Court for Sierra Leone trial judgment](https://www.rscsl.org/Documents/Decisions/Taylor/1283/SCSL-03-01-T-1283.pdf) and [appeal judgment](https://www.rscsl.org/Documents/Decisions/Taylor/Appeal/1389/SCSL-03-01-A-1389.pdf) establish the key legal boundary: he was convicted of aiding and abetting crimes by the RUF/AFRC and planning crimes during specified 1998–99 attacks, not of personally committing every crime or exercising proven command responsibility over those fighters. Judges found that his arms, support, and encouragement substantially assisted crimes, with knowledge; the Appeals Chamber upheld the conviction and 50-year sentence. This gives a grounded path from office skills to regional power: procurement experience, factional politics, weapons and supply networks, then public office. Keep allegations about earlier embezzlement labeled as accusations unless independently established.

## Ordinary service and fictional adaptation

The Sierra Leone TRC report offers contextual evidence about ordinary recruits, separate from any individual’s criminal conviction. It describes young men joining the national army for monthly pay amid idleness, disaffection, and bravado; one recruit said a radio advertisement made him think he could do better at the front. Another chapter recounts an officer’s evidence that senior officials used irregular child recruitment and dead soldiers’ roll numbers to pocket pay and benefits for “ghost soldiers” ([TRC recruitment findings](https://hrc-sl.org/PDF/Media/TRCVolume3A.pdf); [TRC on army payroll corruption](https://www.sierraleonetrc.org/index.php/view-report-text-vol-3b/item/volume-three-b-chapter-four)). These are commission findings and testimony about one conflict, not universal rules about soldiers.

## Fictional beat patterns supported by these records

- A specialist proposes a “practical” policy, proves it can be repeated, earns promotion, then applies it at larger scale.
- A local official holds real but limited authority. Show the protection request, the meeting where it is refused, the order passed down, and the official’s next public appearance.
- A clerk, teacher, or supply officer brings a civilian skill into an institution: files are marked, lists checked, staff trained, stores accounted for. Show how that skill can make a harmful system more efficient.
- A leader can help violence without standing at its scene: financing, weapons, transport, propaganda, official recognition, or help crossing a border can each be a concrete act.
- Let ordinary enlistment begin with a monthly wage, family pressure, status, or a chance at a career; those motives do not predict what the recruit will do. Then give the officer a specific order to obey, question, or refuse.
- In a chain of command, show both pressure and decision: the threat, the subordinate’s options, the act, and what follows. A court may treat pressure as mitigation while still finding guilt.
- Make corruption concrete and ordinary: a commander alters ration records, diverts fuel, skims payroll, or assigns a relative to a paid post. Keep such details fictional unless a case record establishes them.
- Separate a person’s own explanation from the court’s finding. “I protected my people,” “I obeyed,” and “I was only an administrator” are claims to test against conduct, not narrator verdicts.
