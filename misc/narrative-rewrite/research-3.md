# Research memo: variation across 36 character biographies

## Findings from primary studies

- **Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances (2024), “Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the collective diversity of novel content.”** [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244532/). In a preregistered online experiment, 293 participants wrote eight-sentence stories, with or without access to GPT-4 story ideas. Access to ideas raised third-party novelty/usefulness ratings, especially when participants could request up to five ideas. At the same time, AI-assisted stories were more similar to one another: their embedding similarity to the condition’s average story rose about 9–11% of the human-only range. This is a useful distinction for the campaign: a bio can read well on its own while still repeating the same character pattern across the set. The sample was typical online participants, not professional writers; the task was microfiction, not biographies or a connected history. The model and one-shot idea workflow were from 2024, and the authors caution that longer or more interactive writing could behave differently.

- **Xie et al., PNAS (2025), “Echoes in AI: Quantifying lack of plot diversity in LLM outputs.”** [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12415252/). Across 100 prompts/stories from WritingPrompts and television summaries, GPT-4 and Llama 3 generations repeated plot-level events and combinations more often than the human stories. The paper’s example is concrete: when asked to continue Kafka’s story about a lost traveler and a policeman, GPT-4 repeatedly has the policeman give directions or escort the traveler, while Kafka’s abrupt, evasive ending is absent. This supports checking for repeated *choices and reactions*, not only repeated words. Limits: only two model families and two story datasets; the 100-story sample is modest; a model judges semantic echoes; the authors note possible training-data overlap and limits in that judging method. It does not measure biography quality or prove any particular wording identifies AI authorship.

- **Yunusov, Sidat & Emami, EMNLP (2024), “MirrorStories: Reflecting Diversity through Personalized Narrative Generation with Large Language Models.”** [Paper and PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.382/). Their system generated 1,500 short personalized stories using specified reader attributes (such as age, interests, and identity) while preserving a required moral; the authors report greater textual diversity than generic comparison stories and higher engagement ratings from 26 judges. This is useful counterevidence to a fatalistic reading of homogenization: distinct, concrete inputs and constraints can support varied outputs while the campaign’s shared facts stay fixed. It studied personalization and textual diversity, not occupation/motive diversity across a single roster, and the evaluator group was small.

## Practical guidance for the 36 bios and connected campaign history

1. **Plan the roster before drafting.** Give each character a compact profile card with separate fields for livelihood, immediate want, reason they stay involved, ordinary routine, pressure reaction, and emotional register. Allocate variety across the full roster instead of letting a shared prompt fill every slot with “duty, loss, and resolve.” Include practical, social, curious, affectionate, ambitious, opportunistic, playful, tired, and mundane motives alongside grief or resentment. Avoid duplicating the same combination even when individual fields recur.

2. **Make the difference causal.** Give each person a concrete job or daily responsibility that changes what they notice and what they can do. Vary the job ecology: maintenance, transport, supply, care, food, trade, administration, local services, skilled crafts, farming, military roles, informal work, and people temporarily without work. Then tie a motive to that person’s actual situation. A courier’s urgent debt, a mechanic’s curiosity, a medic’s promise to a neighbor, and a clerk protecting a quiet routine create different decisions even if all four appear in the same conflict.

3. **Assign stress reactions as observable behavior.** For each character, decide what they do first when surprised, contradicted, afraid, or asked to help: check the equipment, bargain, go quiet, make a joke, ask who else is at risk, get angry, leave, improvise, tend to someone, or refuse. Distinct reactions give the campaign history different scene mechanics and emotional colors; an abstract adjective alone does not.

4. **Reserve ordinary life details.** Give each bio one small, non-traumatic fact that can recur naturally: a food habit, a repair project, a pet, a shift routine, a neighbor they help, a game, a collection, or a practical worry. Keep it specific enough to belong to that person. This makes the biography about a life, not just its grievance.

5. **Vary the emotional register and prose movement.** Use the same plain, understandable baseline, but vary how each bio moves: some begin with work, some with a choice, a relationship, a habit, or a mistake. Use a mix of short direct sentences and fuller explanatory ones. Let some characters be warm, funny, impatient, embarrassed, content, selfish, or uncertain. End sections on a fact, unresolved choice, relationship, or next action; do not give every biography a solemn lesson or polished aphorism.

6. **Audit the whole set as a set.** After drafting, place all 36 cards side by side and mark repeated job/motive/reaction combinations, repeated history beats, and endings that turn a concrete event into a grand statement. For any cluster that repeats, change the underlying want or behavior rather than swapping synonyms. Read a few bios aloud back-to-back; if they could trade names and still fit, their lives need more distinct evidence.

These recommendations are an application of the studies, not claims that the studies tested this exact 36-bio workflow. The evidence supports guarding against convergence at the collection level while still expecting AI-assisted prose to be useful at the individual level. It does not establish that a phrase, punctuation habit, or solemn ending proves AI authorship.
