# Research memo 2: editorial habits in character vignettes

## Findings and limits

These are craft judgments and observed tendencies, not a reliable way to identify who wrote a passage. A familiar device can work in a particular character's voice; it becomes tiring when it substitutes for an event or repeats across a set.

- **Moral clarity supplied by the narrator.** Atlantic senior editor Adam Kirsch describes his own ChatGPT story prompts yielding lessons, clean moral reversals, and tidy aphoristic endings. The examples end by explaining what a story means instead of leaving the reader with what happened. This is a first-person prompt experiment and an editorial argument, not a controlled study. For dossiers, let the final line be an action, consequence, or odd practical detail; retain a maxim only when it plausibly belongs to that character. [The Atlantic, “Why AI Is Incorrigibly Didactic”](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/06/ai-writing-style-literature/687536/)
- **Too much polish and balance can flatten voice.** Editor and writer Eve Fairbanks reports seeing submissions with uniform sentence lengths and evenly paced paragraphs, and a tone that felt breezy and grandiose. She argues from editorial experience and personal observation. Treat this as useful warning about repeated cadence across 36 entries, not a rule that polished prose or any single punctuation mark proves AI use. [The Atlantic, “The Biggest Tell That Something Was Written by AI”](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/2026/05/how-to-tell-ai-writing/687345/)
- **Figurative details should do a job.** An editor's guide from Editor World recommends asking what a metaphor contributes, whether it compresses an observation, and whether the detail is specific enough to picture. Decorative imagery and objects carrying announced symbolism can draw attention away from the scene. This is editorial craft advice, not evidence specific to AI. Applied to the examples supplied: “every objection to it is weather” compresses an attitude but leaves the actual objections and response unknown; a mundane object should enter because a person used, kept, lost, or damaged it. [Editor World, “Figurative Language in Fiction: An Editor’s Guide”](https://www.editorworld.com/article/figurative-language)
- **Measured language research is narrower than these style claims.** Juzek and Ward’s 2025 COLING paper compared 9,953 ChatGPT-3.5 abstracts with 10,000 PubMed abstracts and analyzed more than 5.2 billion tokens across 26.7 million abstracts. It identified 21 words whose rise in scientific abstracts was plausibly linked to LLM use (including “delve,” “intricate,” and “underscore”), while reporting limits and inconclusive evidence about causes. This supports corpus-level claims about word frequencies in scientific abstracts; it does not validate a fiction detector or establish that opaque aphorisms, object symbolism, or a particular ending are uniquely machine-made. [Juzek & Ward, “Why Does ChatGPT ‘Delve’ So Much?”](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.426/)

## Practical instructions for the 36 dossiers

Write each vignette from a small chain of observable events: who did what, what changed, and what followed. Name the subject and action when the agent matters; replace phrases such as “the correction came” or “the unfolding is practised” with the person who corrected whom, or the repeated task being performed. Give each person a distinct job, want, habit, and pressure; vary which detail gets emphasis and let sentence length follow the event. Avoid making each paragraph arrive at an equally neat, quotable judgment.

Use concrete objects as evidence of a person’s habits or choices, not as automatic symbols. Before keeping an image or aphorism, ask: can I name the exact person, action, or remembered detail it clarifies? If not, replace it with the literal event. State motives and causes plainly when the player needs them. Leave room for uncertainty when the character does not know, rather than hiding causality behind an abstract pronouncement. End on the last useful fact or consequence; do not append a moral explaining its significance.

This is a quality and voice pass, not detector evasion. No ban on metaphors, balanced sentences, or polished lines: use them where a particular person would.

## Tiny examples

- **Before:** “Every objection to it is weather.”  
  **After:** “Mara raised the missing fuel again. Oren wrote ‘discuss later’ beside her name and moved to the next item.”
- **Before:** “The correction, when it came, was brief.”  
  **After:** “At roll call, Vale called him Corporal. He let the title stand until the colonel left.”
