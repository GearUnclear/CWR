# Research memo 4: readable in-game narrative

## Findings for the rewrite

The clearest through-line in the sources is that plain English preserves meaning while reducing the work needed to find it. GOV.UK guidance says to use familiar words, name actions specifically, prefer active voice, and split sentences over 25 words when useful. Its Cabinet Office style guide explicitly says plain English does not mean oversimplifying or changing meaning; it recommends short sentences with one main idea. For a dossier, this supports clarity without flattening grief, blame, fear, or uncertainty: make the event and its consequence legible, then let the emotional weight come from those facts.

For small in-game text panels, use a descriptive heading and put the key fact early. Nielsen Norman Group’s web-reading study found that users often scan, and recommends meaningful subheadings, one idea per paragraph, and the conclusion first. In a dossier this suggests headings such as “The ambush” or “After the ceasefire,” and opening a paragraph with who acted and what changed. A little structure helps players return to the text after interruption. This is an adaptation of web-reading research; it is not direct research on game dossiers.

Keep the actor and action close together. Prefer “The colonel ordered the bridge destroyed” to “The destruction of the bridge was ordered.” Name the cause when it matters: “The radio failed, so the patrol turned east.” Replace abstract event nouns (“the correction,” “the withdrawal,” “the response”) with the person or unit that acted and the specific act. A passive sentence can still work when the actor is unknown or the result matters more than who caused it; otherwise, it can hide responsibility or make readers reconstruct the event.

Use concrete nouns and familiar verbs, while keeping setting terms that carry real meaning. Define a necessary unfamiliar term briefly on first use. Avoid stock, inflated or conspicuously polished wording that delays the fact. Nielsen and Morkes’ web studies found that concise, scannable, objective versions improved usability and readers’ task performance; their account attributes some of the benefit to removing promotional language that makes readers filter hype. For fictional military history, this supports a restrained voice, not a flat one.

For the example “The correction, when it came, was brief,” the reader must infer what was corrected, who corrected it, and what “brief” refers to. A clearer version could be: “At dawn, command corrected the map. The company had entered the valley the night before.” The sequence is plain, while the second sentence keeps the cost and regret in the implication. Short sentences need not repeat the same construction: vary their length when a longer sentence carries a meaningful relationship such as cause, contrast, or consequence.

## Practical checklist

- Can the reader identify the person, unit, or institution doing the important action?
- Does the first sentence state the paragraph’s main event or claim?
- Are cause, timing, and consequence explicit where the reader needs them?
- Can abstract nouns or passive phrasing be replaced with a concrete actor and verb without losing a deliberate ambiguity?
- Are most words familiar? Is any needed military or setting term explained on first use?
- Does each paragraph have one clear job, with a descriptive heading if the panel needs navigation?
- Does the prose sound like a person recording events, rather than a narrator arranging a polished turn of phrase?
- Does the rewrite preserve the actual emotional or factual complexity, instead of cutting a necessary distinction to chase a score?
- Read it once as a player returning after interruption: can you quickly recover who did what and why it mattered?

## Readability scores: useful limits

Treat Flesch-Kincaid or similar scores as a rough diagnostic for long or tangled sentences, not as a pass/fail target for a short campaign history. CDC says formulas estimate difficulty from features such as word and sentence length, but do not measure comprehension or account for layout; pursuing a lower score can remove concepts, omit definitions, or create choppy prose. A CDC/NCHS paper reports that readability formulas become unreliable below 100 words for survey passages, while noting that Fry recommends other checks for shorter passages. That finding concerns surveys, so apply it cautiously to game prose, but an 80–100-word history is still a poor place to treat one grade score as definitive. Long unit, rank, and place names can also raise formula scores. A human read-through against the checklist is more informative; if available, ask a few representative players what happened and why after reading once.

## Sources

- [GOV.UK: Use clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) — familiar words, specific wording, active voice, short paragraphs and sentence guidance.
- [Cabinet Office: Functional Standards writing style guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/handbook-for-standard-managers/functional-standards-writing-style-guide) — plain English without changing meaning; short sentences, one idea per sentence.
- [Nielsen Norman Group: How Users Read on the Web](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/) — 1997 study and practical recommendations for scannability and paragraph focus.
- [Morkes and Nielsen: Applying Writing Guidelines to Web Pages](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/applying-writing-guidelines-web-pages/) — study of concise, scannable, objective web content and task performance.
- [CDC: Get to the Point—Write for Immediate Understanding](https://www.cdc.gov/nceh/clearwriting/mod2/index.html) — readability formulas as rough aids, with examples of how low scores can distort meaning.
- [CDC: Testing for Readability](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/11938/cdc_11938_DS1.pdf) — formulas do not measure comprehension or layout, and long proper names can distort the score.
- [CDC archive / NCHS: Seventh Conference on Health Survey Research Methods](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/nchs/data/slaits/conf07.pdf) — discussion of formula methods and passage-length considerations (PDF; see section around printed pages 84–88).
