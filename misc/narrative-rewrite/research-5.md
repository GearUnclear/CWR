# Short-form game character writing — research memo 5

## Findings for the rewrite

A 35–45 word dossier is a useful production constraint, not a formula validated by the sources. In that space, give the reader a playable person rather than a miniature lore entry: a specific job or role, a present want, one choice or tactic they use, and one ordinary habit that makes them memorable. Make details do double duty. A habit can reveal priorities; a work detail can shape the way they solve a problem. Avoid biography that does not affect a decision, current play, or a relationship.

Keep story attached to action. Supergiant’s Greg Kasavin describes Bastion’s player actions as the text and dialogue as subtext: narration should add intent, history, or context the player could not get from merely seeing the action. He also argues for concise lines, grounded in the current surroundings, and warns that repeated reactive lines break the feeling of a continuous story. For dossiers, this means make a playable decision or behavior the center; use a spare line of description to color it rather than restating the plot. [Supergiant Games, “In-Depth: Writing Bastion”](https://www.supergiantgames.com/blog/in-depth-writing-bastion/)

Distinctness comes from a specific combination, not a stack of dramatic adjectives. In Watch Dogs: Legion, Ubisoft’s narrative team used job, age, background, personal history, quirks, and authored persona/voice variations to make generated citizens legible. The team intentionally added odd details because they stick in memory. Their account also shows the limit: procedurally generated characters lacked the specificity to carry the main plot, so authored “Anchor” characters supplied continuity, shared memory, and concrete opposition. [Game Developer, “How Watch Dogs: Legion changed Ubisoft’s narrative design”](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/how-i-watch-dogs-legion-i-made-ubisoft-rethink-its-storytelling)

Procedural combinations need a world model, not unrestricted mixing. Watch Dogs: Legion’s developers describe using a “Census” knowledge base to connect occupation, income, residence, relationships, activities, and backstory. Constraints prevent both random “procedural soup” and one-note occupational stereotypes: a detail should fit the world, while people sharing a job still differ in schedules, ties, and behavior. The same interview describes writing multiple persona-specific versions of mission lines, then revising them against the original intent. [Game Developer / AI and Games, “How Watch Dogs: Legion’s ‘Play as Anyone’ Simulation Works”](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-watch-dogs-legion-s-play-as-anyone-simulation-works)

## Prompt-ready guidance

Write each character dossier in 35–45 words. Use plain, concrete language. Include: (1) their specific job or role in the current situation, (2) what they want right now, (3) a choice, tactic, or playable behavior that shows how they pursue it, and (4) one ordinary habit or oddly specific detail. Make every detail support a decision, relationship, or action the player can encounter. Keep backstory to one relevant fact at most. Use different wants, methods, habits, and sentence rhythms across the cast; do not make every person sound like the same narrator. Let gameplay show obvious actions; add only subtext or context. Do not use cinematic slogans, moral lessons, generic trauma, abstract trait piles, or lore that changes nothing in play. For procedural variants, preserve shared world facts and the character’s core want; check that job, means, relationships, schedule, knowledge, and dialogue fit together. Vary voice through a defined persona, not random quirks. Give major plot continuity to authored characters or stable shared events.
