# Grounding ordinary lives inside resistance movements

These accounts ground fictional people in a pressure, relationship, or job. They are not interchangeable: Jewish undergrounds faced extermination and displacement; the ANC worked under apartheid; the NLF operated in South Vietnam. These are later recollections. I label archive context as summary and adaptation ideas as inference. Keep real names in research only.

## Kraków: an artist in the underground press

John Komski was a Polish art student in Kraków when Germany invaded in 1939. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum says food scarcity and queues shaped the city as Komski decided to join the resistance. In his oral-history excerpt, he describes listening to forbidden radio, building membership, and producing a handmade newsletter. His art training let him compose and letter pages by hand. He and two friends later tried to escape toward France, were caught, and he was imprisoned at Auschwitz as a political prisoner. The museum summary links food scarcity and his decision; his own excerpt describes his work. [USHMM oral history: John Komski](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/oral-history/john-komski-describes-resistance-activities-in-krakow-including-an-underground-newspaper)

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** Give a recruit a practical first task that uses an existing skill—hand lettering, copying notices, fixing a radio—then let the cost of that work emerge through food queues, cramped rooms, and the decision to flee. Avoid turning “complaints” into a complete motive without showing the specific conditions and choice.

## Warsaw: a courier who carried an ordinary-looking nuisance

Vladka Meed was a member of the Jewish Fighting Organization (ZOB) and the Bund youth movement in the Warsaw ghetto. In December 1942 she was smuggled to the non-ghetto side to seek arms and hiding places for people at risk; she later carried messages and materials between ghettos, camps, and forests. In her interview, she recalls waiting at a railway station with false documents, photographs, and letters hidden under a double collar—and three live chicks on top of them. A railway worker helped her onto a train after mistaking her for a young woman visiting family. The chickens were both cover and a real, inconvenient object she had to manage while she waited. [USHMM oral history: Vladka Meed](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/oral-history/vladka-fagele-peltel-meed-describes-waiting-at-a-train-station-with-false-documents-to-be-smuggled-into-the-warsaw-ghetto)

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** A courier’s tension can come from ordinary transport and a cover story that needs to hold up in conversation. Make clear what is hidden, who needs it, and why the courier has to make this trip; the funny or awkward cover detail should not erase the lethal stakes.

## The Netherlands: a medical student’s delivery route

Tina Strobos was a medical student when Germany invaded the Netherlands. She and sorority members joined the underground; she hid Jews, found children places to stay, helped with false papers, and carried items for the resistance. Her examples include picking up a child and making trips with a radio transmitter or stolen weapons. Checkpoints could take her food or bicycle. Curfew made each return a calculation: if she could not get home by eight, she might move from doorway to doorway at night to find shelter. She also recalled the danger that authorities could shoot prisoners in reprisals after resistance attacks. [USHMM oral history: Tina Strobos](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/oral-history/tina-strobos-describes-her-courier-duties-for-the-underground-in-the-netherlands). The exact-title search result returned the oral-history page, transcript, and USHMM collection attribution; the browser’s direct-open 502 was a fetch error.

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** Show how one errand competes with another—deliver a device, keep a child safe, get home before curfew, and protect the bicycle needed for tomorrow’s work. The pressure is logistical as well as ideological.

## Naroch Forest: protection mixed with coercion

Alisa (Lisa) Nussbaum Derman escaped the Slonim ghetto, traveled through Grodno and Vilna, and joined a resistance group whose partisans operated from the Naroch Forest. Her testimony describes attacks on German movement and efforts to help Jews—children, older people, and women—who had fled into the woods. She says the partisans tried to make farmers shelter and watch over those fugitives, but also says they threatened severe punishment if the farmers failed to keep them safe. That detail matters: her account presents protection as real, while also showing the armed group imposing its will on rural people. The people being protected were often short of food and moving from hiding place to hiding place; some died. [USHMM oral history: Alisa Nussbaum Derman](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/oral-history/alisa-lisa-nussbaum-derman-describes-partisan-activities)

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** If fighters requisition food or shelter, make the need and the dispute concrete. A farmer can fear both the armed visitors and the patrols that may follow them. A character may protect someone while resenting the method used to secure that protection. Do not make refusal or compliance a simple test of goodness.

## Lublin district: a forest camp’s daily system

Frank Bleichman said deportations from the Lublin area led him to join Jewish partisans searching the forests for weapons and food. His account of a camp of about one hundred people is practical: they lived in bunkers without water, lights, or sanitation; two people fetched water each day; the group watched the forest edge; and they slept in their trousers so they could move quickly. A messenger warned another camp when a raid began. Bleichman and a few others escaped, returned later, buried the dead, and said Kaddish. The testimony gives routines and decisions rather than a polished picture of an organized army. [USHMM oral history: Frank Bleichman](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/oral-history/frank-bleichman-describes-conditions-in-and-dangers-facing-a-partisan-camp-in-the-forests-of-poland)

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** Establish who fetches water, who keeps watch, who carries a warning, and what happens when one of them is missing. Those jobs can produce conflict and attachment before a character ever fires a weapon.

## Durban: a student organizer pulled deeper underground

In a 2002 interview, South African activist Claudia Manning traced her political involvement to conversations about poverty, unemployment, and political imprisonment in her Durban community. As a teenager she joined student organizing; later she mobilized residents around education, housing, and wages, then distributed ANC pamphlets after an activist approached her. During the 1986 state of emergency, her mother was detained and Manning hid for months while attending university. A police raid exposed her through a former primary-school classmate. She gave a false name and threw her library cards in a bathroom; she was held in solitary confinement for three months at age twenty. At first her only reading was a Bible; after a prison officer noticed her distress, she got novels, including *The Catcher in the Rye*. After release, the ANC recruited her into intelligence work. She translated and typed information, analyzed who might be informing on activists, and used safe houses. [O’Malley archive interview with Claudia Manning](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/cis/omalley/OMalleyWeb/03lv03445/04lv03833/05lv03858/06lv03878.htm)

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** Let commitment grow through a sequence: a local issue, a low-risk task, a family member’s detention, then a decision made under pressure. Even a clandestine character still has classes, errands, neighbors, and a reading life. Do not imply arrest proves recruitment was inevitable; Manning describes recruitment after release as a subsequent decision by the organization.

## Saigon: a student recruited after a demonstration

The PBS *Vietnam Passage* account presents Tao as a middle-class schoolgirl whose mother taught her to oppose repression. Tao says she and her family joined demonstrations after the Diem government’s repression of Buddhists. A Viet Cong recruiter noticed Tao and her sister at a rally and later visited their home; the sisters joined the guerrilla movement. At eighteen, Tao took part in a plan involving the Saigon central police station and a staff member she hoped to recruit; she says that person betrayed her, and she was arrested. The account also records her postwar choice to study marine biology because she thought it could help feed the country. This is a distinct route into armed resistance: family political example and a public demonstration, followed by a recruiter’s visit. [PBS, *Vietnam Passage*: Tao](https://www.pbs.org/vietnampassage/Stories/stories.tao.02.html)

**Date check:** The PBS page’s first-person text says the Buddhist repression happened “in 1964,” but this conflicts with the U.S. State Department’s *Foreign Relations of the United States* chronology, which dates the beginning of the Buddhist Crisis to May 9, 1963, and its record of Diem’s death in November 1963. Treat 1964 as a PBS page error; the paragraph above therefore leaves the demonstration undated. [Office of the Historian, Buddhist Crisis chronology](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v03/p_NDD1) · [Office of the Historian, November 1963 record](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v04/d301)

**Fictional adaptation beat (inference):** A recruit can begin as a demonstrator, learn a movement through family or school, and then be approached by someone who gives a specific next task. Show the shift from public protest to secrecy as a change in daily habits, trust, and risk. Give the character a future interest beyond the movement.

For a grounded character, name the immediate need and task: someone to hide, a letter, water, papers, or a class. Motives can coexist with fear, family obligations, mistrust, coercion, and ordinary pleasure. Show who asked, why the character agreed or refused, and what changed the next day.
