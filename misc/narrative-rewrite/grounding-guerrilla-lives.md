# Grounding fictional guerrilla biographies in documented lives

This memo collects eight individual histories for building readable fictional backgrounds. Each “Documented” paragraph is grounded in the linked testimony, memoir, archive, or museum record. “Fiction cue” is my craft inference, not a claim about what the historical person intended. These people belonged to distinct organizations and situations; no one story represents a whole movement.

## 1. Faye Schulman: photographer turned improvised nurse

**Documented.** Before the German occupation, Faye learned photography from her brother and helped in his studio in Lenin, Poland. In August 1942 German forces murdered her parents and siblings with most of the town’s Jews. They spared Faye because she could develop photographs and ordered her to process images of the massacre; she secretly kept copies. She escaped to the forest during a partisan raid and joined the Molotava Brigade, made mostly of escaped Soviet POWs. The group accepted her because it needed medical help. Faye had no medical training; the doctor was a veterinarian. She nursed from 1942 to 1944, recovered her camera, hid it on missions, and developed film under blankets. Her photographs also document antisemitism within the group and a joint Jewish-Russian partisan funeral. The USHMM profile is authored by the Jewish Partisan Educational Foundation (JPEF); JPEF also preserves her photographs and a filmed interview. [USHMM/JPEF profile](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/faye-schulman); [JPEF exhibit](https://www.jewishpartisans.org/pictures-of-resistance).

**Fiction cue (inference):** A person’s old skill may lead to an improvised second job. Show what they can do, what they lack, and how dependence can coexist with prejudice.

## 2. Rae Kushner: family survivor doing camp work

**Documented.** Rae was one of four children in a family whose home and business Soviet authorities took after occupying eastern Poland. In the Novogrudok ghetto, her mother and older sister were killed. Rae escaped with her father, younger sister, and others through a tunnel prisoners dug; darkness and gunfire followed, and her brother died during the escape. After ten days in the woods, the family spent a week in an acquaintance’s cow stable. The Bielski camp admitted them. Rae stood guard and cooked potatoes, soup, and bread for a camp of more than 1,200. She later reconnected with Joseph, whom she had known before the war, and they married after liberation. JPEF says its profiles draw on interviews and archives; its page links Rae’s interview transcripts, including testimony about hunger. [JPEF biography and transcripts](https://www.jewishpartisans.org/partisans/rae-kushner); [USHMM profile](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/rae-kushner).

**Fiction cue (inference):** Say clearly whether someone joins as a fighter, escapes with relatives, or is admitted as a civilian. Guard shifts and thin meals can define camp life as much as raids.

## 3. Vladka Meed: courier with an unresolved family loss

**Documented.** Vladka was a teenager in Warsaw and belonged to the Bund’s Zukunft youth movement. She joined the Jewish Fighting Organization in the ghetto. In her USHMM oral history, she recalls trying to bribe a policeman to rescue her mother and brother at the deportation assembly point; she had only a small watch and a few belongings. She promised to go with them, but could not make herself board the train, a decision she said still troubled her. The underground later smuggled her to the Polish side of Warsaw to obtain weapons and hiding places. She worked as a courier among the underground, people in hiding, labor camps, and forest partisans. Her memoir first appeared in Yiddish in 1948. [USHMM testimony](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/oral-history/vladka-fagele-peltel-meed-describes-the-deportation-of-her-mother-and-brother-from-the-warsaw-ghetto-to-treblinka); [USHMM memoir description](https://shop.ushmm.org/products/on-both-sides-of-the-wall-memoirs-from-the-warsaw-ghetto).

**Fiction cue (inference):** A character can carry regret about an earlier choice while continuing useful work. State what she tried, why it failed, and what she did afterward.

## 4. Zofia Yamaika: staying because escape endangered her parents

**Documented.** The USHMM records Zofia as a teenager from a well-to-do Hasidic family in Warsaw. Uneasy about Polish-Jewish tension, she joined the communist student club Spartacus. After the occupation ended her schooling, she helped revive the banned club, printing and distributing anti-fascist leaflets. She trained with a smuggled pistol and wanted to leave the ghetto, but feared that escape would endanger her parents. After they were deported in July 1942, she escaped and joined the Lion partisans near Radom. During a German attack, Zofia and two Polish comrades covered their unit’s retreat. She died at eighteen; the unit escaped. This is an individual museum ID-card record, not a description of all Warsaw resistance. [USHMM “Armed Jewish Resistance” gallery](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/gallery/armed-jewish-resistance-partisans-stories).

**Fiction cue (inference):** Delayed action can have a legible reason: she stays because leaving risks her parents, then leaves when that constraint changes.

## 5. Rubin Segalowicz: painter caught in reprisal violence

**Documented.** Rubin grew up in Ivenets, Poland, one of four children of a butcher. After studying at a yeshiva, he earned a living as a house painter. The museum record notes that locals drove customers away from Jewish stores before the war. After deportation to Novogrudok, Rubin escaped and joined Soviet partisans. One winter patrol entered a village to retaliate against people aiding the Germans; the partisans killed them and burned part of the town. On retreat, a comrade tried to take a man’s boots and discovered he was a German soldier. The group ran for a river and escaped because it had frozen overnight. The account records civilian occupation, reprisal, and a foolish near-fatal mistake together. [USHMM gallery record](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/gallery/armed-jewish-resistance-partisans-stories).

**Fiction cue (inference):** Let characters do harm as well as suffer it. Name who acted, who was hurt, and the consequence; vague “incidents” obscure moral choices.

## 6. Ada Gobetti: teacher, mother, organizer

**Documented.** Ada worked as a teacher and translator and had been active in anti-fascist circles for years. After the 1943 armistice, she and her son Paolo participated in the Italian Resistance in Piedmont. Her diary, later published as *Partisan Diary*, recorded daily events and her worry about sons leaving on sabotage missions, including her own. A scholarly biography notes that when first asked to organize a women’s resistance group, Ada said she had not paid attention to women’s issues; she later organized women and an underground newspaper. The Centro Studi Piero Gobetti holds her papers, including Resistance documents and political leaflets. [Centro Studi archive finding aid](https://www.centrogobetti.it/images/stories/Archivio/AdaGobetti.htm); [Oxford edition of her diary](https://books.google.com/books?id=_GzrAwAAQBAJ); [JSTOR scholarly biography](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.7722/j.ctt2111fkx).

**Fiction cue (inference):** Let commitment grow from familiar duties. A parent can organize a network and still fear for a child; a support role can become political work without a sudden personality change.

## 7. Yay Panlilio: a reporter whose solo resistance was failing

**Documented.** Yay Panlilio was a Filipina-Irish American journalist who moved from the United States to Manila. Her 1950 autobiography describes her as a reporter and US Army agent. Seven months after Pearl Harbor, Japanese authorities had put a price on her head. Alone in a remote farm hut, she was ill with malaria. She writes that she had tried to resist on her own but found it impossible; guerrillas arrived, and she joined the Marking group. This is Panlilio’s own account, written several years later, and its romantic framing should be kept in mind. [Panlilio’s memoir, *The Crucible* (JSTOR record and chapter-one excerpt)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt5hj3tw); [Australian War Memorial catalog record](https://www.awm.gov.au/collection/LIB10436).

**Fiction cue (inference):** Make a turning point concrete: illness, isolation, and wanted status can make solo action untenable; the group first offers shelter.

## 8. Muthoni wa Kirima: married farm worker in the forest

**Documented.** In a 2019 interview conducted in Gikuyu and translated by Angela Kariuki for the Museum of British Colonialism, Muthoni recalled harvesting pyrethrum for settlers before joining Mau Mau around 1950. She could not remember the exact start; she said she left home for the forest in 1952 with her husband. She described inadequate clothes and shoes, rain, hunger, and loss of village food and information after residents were forced into fenced villages. The fighters foraged crops; she recalled eating raw cabbage and maize. She also said some former comrades surrendered and later hunted the forest group alongside colonial forces. Muthoni recalled her own encounters with settlers as aimed at making them leave, not harming them; that is her account, not a complete description of Mau Mau conduct. The transcript also records small routines: silence by day, songs at night, and her view that caves were traps. [Full oral-history transcript and provenance](https://museumofbritishcolonialism.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/EmergencyStories_MuthoniWaKirima.pdf).

**Fiction cue (inference):** Let a veteran have uncertain dates and reject familiar myths. Show dependence on neighbors, the hardship when that support disappears, and the habits people use to keep going.

## Practical takeaway

For clarity, give each person an ordinary life, a specific pressure that changes it, an actual task, and one continuing personal concern. Say who made each decision and what it cost. A cook, courier, nurse, spy, and fighter have different routines. Let people care, fear, make mistakes, disagree, and sometimes cause harm; draw each detail from that person’s circumstances rather than treating them as a movement’s representative.
